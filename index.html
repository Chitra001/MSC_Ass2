1 · Frequency Allocation for Satellite Services

Frequency allocation for satellite services is an intricate process coordinated and managed globally by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) to prevent signal interference across national borders.

  • It is divided into three geographic regions.
  • It uses multiple radio bands — L, S, C, X, Ku and Ka — to support services such as Fixed Satellite Service (FSS), Broadcasting Satellite Service (BSS) and Mobile Satellite Service (MSS).
  • In India, these allocations are governed by the WPC Wing under the National Frequency Allocation Plan (NFAP).

Key Regulatory Bodies

International Telecommunication Union (ITU)

A United Nations agency that coordinates global radio spectrum and satellite orbits across international borders.

National Authorities (e.g., WPC in India)

Local governments adapt international treaties into national plans (such as India's National Frequency Allocation Plan) to license and manage spectrum inside their borders.

3 · Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

Kepler's laws describe planetary motion. They are three scientific rules discovered by the German astronomer Johannes Kepler between 1609 and 1621. They state that:

  1. Planets orbit the Sun in ellipses,
  2. They sweep out equal areas in equal times,
  3. Their orbital periods are proportional to the cube of their distance.

First Law — Law of Orbits

A planet orbits the Sun in an ellipse, with the Sun located at one of the two foci. The closest point in the orbit is the perihelion and the farthest point is the aphelion.

Semi-major axis from apsides
a = rmin + rmax2

Second Law — Law of Equal Areas

The line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal time intervals. This means planets travel faster when closer to the Sun (perihelion) and slower when farther away (aphelion).

Areal velocity (constant)
ΔAΔt = constant

Third Law — Law of Periods

The square of the orbital period T of a planet is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis a of its orbit.

Kepler's third law (general)
T2 = 4π2GM a3
Ratio form
T2 = k · a3   or   T12T22 = a13a23

Simplified Solar-System Form

If expressed in the following units:

  • T in Earth years
  • a in Astronomical Units (AU)  (a = 1 AU for Earth)
  • M in Solar masses (M☉)

then 4π²/G = 1, and the law can be expressed simply as:

Simplified third law
T2 = a3
T — orbital period (Earth years)
a — semi-major axis (AU)
G — gravitational constant (6.674×10−11 m3·kg−1·s−2)
M — mass of the central body (kg, or M☉ in solar units)
k — proportionality constant (= 1 in solar units)

4 · Orbital Elements (Six Keplerian Parameters)

To visualize how a satellite moves in space, engineers use six Keplerian orbital elements to define its path, look angles to point ground-station antennas toward it, and orbital perturbations (like Earth's oblateness, or the J2 effect) that cause these parameters to change over time.

When visualizing these elements, we reference the Geocentric Equatorial Inertial (ECI) frame, where the Earth's equatorial plane acts as the base reference plane.

Orbital elements are the six parameters needed to uniquely define and describe the path (orbit) of one object moving around another in space — such as a planet around the Sun, or a satellite around the Earth. In satellite communication they mathematically describe and uniquely specify the size, shape, orientation and position of a satellite's orbit.

1 · Semi-Major Axis (a) — SIZE

Measures the size of the orbit — half of the longest diameter of an ellipse (stretching from perigee to apogee through the centre of the Earth). For a circular orbit, it is simply the radius (a = r). It determines the satellite's orbital period and average distance from Earth.

2 · Eccentricity (e) — SHAPE

Describes how stretched or round the orbit is:

  • e = 0 — perfectly circular orbit
  • 0 < e < 1 — elliptical orbit (most common)
  • e = 1 — parabolic trajectory (unbound path)
  • e > 1 — hyperbolic trajectory (unbound path)
3 · Inclination (i) — TILT

Measures the vertical tilt of the orbital plane relative to a reference plane (Earth's equatorial plane or the Sun's ecliptic), ranging from 0° to 180°, measured at the ascending node:

  • 0° — equatorial orbit
  • 90° — polar orbit
  • 0° ≤ i < 90° — prograde orbit (rotates in the same direction as Earth)
  • 90° < i ≤ 180° — retrograde orbit (rotates opposite to Earth)
4 · Right Ascension of the Ascending Node (Ω / RAAN) — TWIST

Defines the horizontal orientation of the orbital plane in space. It is the angle measured eastward in the equatorial plane from the vernal equinox (first point of Aries) to the ascending node — the point where the satellite crosses the equator moving from south to north.

5 · Argument of Perigee (ω) — ROTATION

Defines the orientation of the ellipse within its orbital plane. It is the angle measured along the orbital path from the ascending node to the perigee (the point where the satellite is closest to Earth).

6 · Mean Anomaly (M) / True Anomaly (ν) — POSITION

Defines the position of the satellite along its orbit at a specific reference time (epoch). It describes the fractional progress of the satellite around its orbital period relative to perigee:

  • 0° at perigee
  • 180° at apogee
  • 360° after a full revolution
Standard element set (units)
a [m]  ·  e [–]  ·  i [rad]
ω [rad]  ·  Ω [rad]  ·  M [rad]

All six orbital-element concepts and analysis are applicable to orbits such as LEO, MEO and GEO.

5 · Why Orbital Elements Matter in Satellite Communication

Prediction

Once you know these six values for a satellite or planet, you can calculate where it was in the past and where it will be in the future.

Standard Formats

They are frequently packed into standard data strings like TLEs (Two-Line Element sets) used across aerospace and astronomy, tracking software and ground networks.

Antenna Tracking ("Look Angles")

Ground stations use these elements to calculate precise azimuth and elevation angles so directional antennas can track and communicate with the satellite — essential for moving LEO or HEO satellites.

Satellite Identification

They distinguish different satellites sharing similar orbital regimes and allow operators to predict future positions.

Handover & Coverage

Knowing the semi-major axis and inclination helps predict footprint coverage, signal delay and handovers between constellation nodes.

Perturbation Tracking

Real-world forces — atmospheric drag, solar radiation pressure and Earth's oblateness — continuously alter these elements, requiring regular Two-Line Element (TLE) updates.

6 · Orbital Perturbations & Visualising the Orbit

In a simple circular orbit (as sketched in the notes), the satellite moves with a tangential velocity while the gravitational force points toward the Earth's centre, keeping it on a path of constant altitude. In reality, the six Keplerian elements are not fixed — they drift.

  • Earth's oblateness (J2) — the dominant perturbation; causes the orbital plane and the line of apsides to precess (drift) over time.
  • Atmospheric drag — significant in LEO; shrinks the semi-major axis and lowers the orbit.
  • Solar radiation pressure — small but persistent, especially for large-area spacecraft.
  • Third-body (lunisolar) gravity — the Moon and Sun slowly change a satellite's inclination and node; dominant for GEO satellites.

These effects are why the six orbital elements must be refreshed regularly (via TLEs), and why GEO satellites require north–south station-keeping (see the worked example in the Numericals mode).

7 · Kepler's Laws — Solved Numericals

Numerical 1 — Orbital Period of an Asteroid (Solar System)

Question: An asteroid is discovered orbiting the Sun at an average distance (semi-major axis) of 4 AU. How many Earth years does it take for this asteroid to complete one full orbit around the Sun?

Step 1 — Choose the simplified third law

When an object orbits our Sun, we use Earth's metrics as a baseline (TEarth = 1 year, aEarth = 1 AU). This simplifies Kepler's Third Law directly to:

Governing equation
T2 = a3
T — orbital period of the asteroid (Earth years)
a — semi-major axis of the orbit (AU)
TEarth — baseline period = 1 year
aEarth — baseline distance = 1 AU

Step 2 — Cube the semi-major axis

Substitution
a = 4 AU  →  a3 = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64
a — given semi-major axis (= 4 AU)
a3 — cube of the semi-major axis (AU3)
Result of Step 2
T2 = 64

Step 3 — Take the square root

Governing equation
T = √(a3) = √(T2)
T — orbital period (Earth years)
T2 — squared period (= 64 yr2)
Substitution
T = √64 = 8
Final answer
T = 8 Earth years

Numerical 2 — Finding an Orbital Distance (Exoplanet System)

Question: An exoplanet is detected orbiting a distant star. Space telescopes determine that the planet completes its orbit in 27 Earth years. If the star behaves exactly like our Sun, what is the planet's average distance from it in AU?

Step 1 — Use the simplified Solar-mass relationship

Governing equation
T2 = a3
T — orbital period (Earth years)
a — semi-major axis / average distance (AU)

Step 2 — Square the orbital period

Substitution
T = 27 years  →  T2 = 27 × 27 = 729
T — given orbital period (= 27 years)
T2 — squared period (yr2)
Result of Step 2
729 = a3

Step 3 — Take the cube root

Governing equation
a = ∛(a3) = ∛(T2)
a — semi-major axis (AU)
a3 — cube of semi-major axis (= 729 AU3)
Substitution
a = ∛729 = 9
Final answer
a = 9 AU

Numerical 3 — Kepler's First Law: Semi-Major Axis & Eccentricity

Question: An asteroid moves in an elliptical orbit around the Sun. Its closest distance (perihelion) is rmin = 1.0 AU and its farthest distance (aphelion) is rmax = 3.0 AU. Find the semi-major axis (a) and the eccentricity (e) of the orbit.

Step 1 — Find the semi-major axis

The semi-major axis is the average of the perihelion and aphelion distances.

Governing equation
a = rmin + rmax2
a — semi-major axis (AU)
rmin — perihelion distance (AU)
rmax — aphelion distance (AU)
Substitution
a = 1.0 + 3.02 = 4.02
Result of Step 1
a = 2.0 AU

Step 2 — Find the focal distance c

The distance from the centre of the ellipse to a focus is the semi-major axis minus the perihelion distance.

Governing equation
c = a − rmin
c — focal distance from ellipse centre (AU)
a — semi-major axis (= 2.0 AU)
rmin — perihelion distance (= 1.0 AU)
Substitution
c = 2.0 − 1.0 = 1.0
Result of Step 2
c = 1.0 AU

Step 3 — Compute the eccentricity

Governing equation
e = ca
e — eccentricity (dimensionless, 0 ≤ e < 1)
c — focal distance (= 1.0 AU)
a — semi-major axis (= 2.0 AU)
Substitution
e = 1.02.0 = 0.5
Final answer
a = 2.0 AU   and   e = 0.5

Numerical 4 — Kepler's Second Law: Equal Areas

Question: A planet takes 5 days to sweep out a triangular area A1 = 2.0 × 1012 km2 near its perihelion. How much area (A2) does it sweep out in 5 days when it is near its aphelion?

Step 1 — State the law of equal areas

Governing equation
ΔAΔt = constant  ⟹  A1Δt1 = A2Δt2
ΔA — area swept out by the radius vector (km2)
Δt — time interval (days)
A1 — area swept near perihelion (= 2.0 × 1012 km2)
A2 — area swept near aphelion (km2)
Δt1 — first time interval (= 5 days)
Δt2 — second time interval (= 5 days)

Step 2 — Apply the equal-time condition

The areal velocity is constant, and since the time interval is equal in both cases (5 days), the swept area must also be equal:

Substitution
Δt1 = Δt2 = 5 days  ⟹  A2 = A1
A2 — unknown swept area (km2)
A1 — known swept area (= 2.0 × 1012 km2)
Final answer
A2 = A1 = 2.0 × 1012 km2

Numerical 5 — Kepler's Third Law: Orbital Period of Mars

Question: Mars has a semi-major axis of aMars = 1.524 AU. Using Earth as a baseline (aEarth = 1.0 AU, TEarth = 1 year), calculate the orbital period of Mars in Earth years.

Step 1 — Write the ratio form of the third law

Governing equation
( TMarsTEarth )2 = ( aMarsaEarth )3
TMars — orbital period of Mars (Earth years)
TEarth — orbital period of Earth (= 1 year)
aMars — semi-major axis of Mars (= 1.524 AU)
aEarth — semi-major axis of Earth (= 1.0 AU)

Step 2 — Substitute the known values

Substitution
TMars2 = (1.524)3 ≈ 3.5396
TMars2 — squared orbital period (yr2)
1.524 — ratio aMars/aEarth (dimensionless)
Result of Step 2
TMars2 ≈ 3.5396 yr2

Step 3 — Take the square root

Governing equation
TMars = √(TMars2)
TMars — orbital period (Earth years)
TMars2 — squared period (= 3.5396 yr2)
Substitution
TMars = √3.5396 ≈ 1.88
Final answer
TMars ≈ 1.88 Earth years (≈ 687 days)

8 · Solved Example: Cartesian Vectors → Classical Orbital Elements

To determine the shape, orientation and real-time position of a spacecraft, we convert its Cartesian position vector r⃗ and velocity vector v⃗ into the six Classical Orbital Elements (COEs):

  1. a — size of the orbit
  2. e — shape of the orbit
  3. i — tilt of the orbital plane relative to the equator
  4. Ω — twist (RAAN) of the orbital plane
  5. ω — orientation of the orbit within its plane
  6. ν — position of the satellite along the path
Problem statement: A satellite orbiting Earth has the following position and velocity vectors in the Geocentric Equatorial Inertial (GEI) frame:

r⃗ = 6500 î + 3400 ĵ + 4100 k̂  (km)
v⃗ = −4.9 î + 5.2 ĵ + 3.4 k̂  (km/s)

Calculate the 6 Classical Orbital Elements (a, e, i, Ω, ω, ν).
Given Earth's gravitational parameter: μ = 3.986004 × 106 km3/s2.

Step 1 — Calculate Magnitudes and Basic Helper Scalars

Governing equations
r = √(x2 + y2 + z2)
v = √(vx2 + vy2 + vz2)
vr = r⃗ · v⃗r
x, y, z — position components (km) = 6500, 3400, 4100
vx, vy, vz — velocity components (km/s) = −4.9, 5.2, 3.4
r — position magnitude (km)
v — velocity magnitude (km/s)
vr — radial velocity component (km/s)
r⃗ · v⃗ — dot product (km2/s)
Substitution
r = √(65002 + 34002 + 41002)
  = √(42 250 000 + 11 560 000 + 16 810 000) = √70 620 000

v = √((−4.9)2 + 5.22 + 3.42)
  = √(24.01 + 27.04 + 11.56) = √62.61

r⃗ · v⃗ = (6500 × −4.9) + (3400 × 5.2) + (4100 × 3.4)
  = −31 850 + 17 680 + 13 940 = −230
Result of Step 1
r = 8403.57 km  ·  v = 7.9126 km/s
r⃗ · v⃗ = −230 km2/s  (negative ⇒ flying toward perigee)

Step 2 — Semi-Major Axis (a)

Specific mechanical energy & semi-major axis
E = v22 − μr = − μ2a   ⟹   a = − μ2E
E — specific mechanical energy (km2/s2)
v — velocity magnitude (= 7.9126 km/s)
μ — Earth's gravitational parameter (= 398 600.4 km3/s2)
r — position magnitude (= 8403.57 km)
a — semi-major axis (km)
Substitution
E = 7.912622 − 398 600.48403.57
  = 31.305 − 47.432 = −16.127

a = − 398 600.42 × (−16.127)
Result of Step 2
a ≈ 12 358.1 km

Step 3 — Angular Momentum Vector (h⃗)

Specific angular momentum
h⃗ = r⃗ × v⃗ = det î   ĵ   k̂
x   y   z
vx   vy   vz
h⃗ — specific angular momentum vector (km2/s); defines the orientation of the orbital plane
î, ĵ, k̂ — unit vectors of the GEI frame
x, y, z — position components (km)
vx, vy, vz — velocity components (km/s)
Substitution
hx = (3400 × 3.4) − (4100 × 5.2) = 11 560 − 21 320 = −9760
hy = (4100 × −4.9) − (6500 × 3.4) = −20 090 − 22 100 = −42 190
hz = (6500 × 5.2) − (3400 × −4.9) = 33 800 + 16 660 = 50 460

h = √(hx2 + hy2 + hz2) = √((−9760)2 + (−42 190)2 + 50 4602)
Result of Step 3
h⃗ = −9760 î − 42 190 ĵ + 50 460 k̂  (km2/s)
h = |h⃗| ≈ 66 498 km2/s

Step 4 — Eccentricity (e)

Governing equation
e = √( 1 − h2μ a )
e — eccentricity (dimensionless)
h — angular momentum magnitude (= 66 497.8 km2/s)
μ — gravitational parameter (= 398 600.4 km3/s2)
a — semi-major axis (= 12 358.1 km)
Substitution
e = √( 1 − (66 497.8)2398 600.4 × 12 358.1 )
  = √( 1 − 4.4219 × 1094.9259 × 109 )
  = √(1 − 0.8977) = √0.1023
Result of Step 4
e ≈ 0.320  (elliptical orbit)

Step 5 — Inclination (i)

Governing equation
cos i = hzh
i — inclination (degrees, 0°–180°)
hz — z-component of h⃗ (= 50 460 km2/s)
h — magnitude of h⃗ (= 66 497.8 km2/s)
Substitution
cos i = 50 46066 497.8 ≈ 0.7588
Result of Step 5
i ≈ 40.65°  (prograde orbit)

Step 6 — Right Ascension of the Ascending Node (Ω)

Governing equations
n⃗ = k̂ × h⃗ = (−hy, hx, 0)
Ω = atan2( ny , nx )
n⃗ — node vector, pointing to the ascending node (km2/s)
k̂ — Earth's Z-axis unit vector (0, 0, 1)
hx, hy — components of h⃗ (= −9760, −42 190)
Ω — RAAN measured eastward from vernal equinox (degrees)
Substitution
n⃗ = (42 190, −9760, 0)
Ω = atan2(−9760, 42 190) = −13.03°
Result of Step 6
Ω ≈ 346.97°  (adding 360° to make it positive)

Step 7 — Argument of Perigee (ω)

Governing equations
e⃗ = 1μ [ ( v2 − μr ) r⃗ − ( r⃗ · v⃗ ) v⃗ ]

cos ω = n⃗ · e⃗|n⃗| |e⃗|
e⃗ — eccentricity vector (points to perigee)
v2 — squared speed (= 62.61 km2/s2)
μ/r — gravitational term (= 47.432 km2/s2)
r⃗ · v⃗ — dot product (= −230 km2/s)
ω — argument of perigee (degrees)

e⃗ = 1398 600.4 [ 15.178 (6500, 3400, 4100) + 230 (−4.9, 5.2, 3.4) ]
  = (0.2447, 0.1325, 0.1581)

n⃗ · e⃗ = (42 190 × 0.2447) + (−9760 × 0.1325) ≈ 9030

cos ω = 903043 304 × 0.320 ≈ 0.652
Result of Step 7
ω ≈ 49.3°

Step 8 — True Anomaly (ν)

Governing equation

ν — true anomaly (degrees)
e⃗ — eccentricity vector (= (0.2447, 0.1325, 0.1581))
r⃗ — position vector (= (6500, 3400, 4100) km)
r — position magnitude (= 8403.57 km)
Substitution
e⃗ · r⃗ = (0.2447 × 6500) + (0.1325 × 3400) + (0.1581 × 4100) ≈ 2689
cos ν = 26890.320 × 8403.57 ≈ 0.9999

Sanity check: perigee radius rp = a(1 − e) = 12 358.1 × (1 − 0.320) ≈ 8403 km — essentially equal to the given r. The satellite is right at perigee, which matches ν ≈ 359°.

9 · Third-Body Lunisolar Perturbations (Analytical View)

Problem statement: A communications satellite is parked in a Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO) with a semi-major axis a = 42 164 km, an inclination i = 0° and an eccentricity e = 0. Calculate the worst-case maximum secular rate of change of inclination (di/dt) caused by the Moon's gravitational perturbation over a long period.

Given Constants

ωE — Earth's rotation rate ≈ 7.292 × 10−5 rad/s
n — satellite mean motion = √(μ/a3) ≈ 7.292 × 10−5 rad/s (1 rev/day)
nm — Moon's mean motion around Earth ≈ 2.66 × 10−6 rad/s (27.3 days)
μm* — Moon's mass ratio, MMoon/(MEarth + MMoon) ≈ 0.0123
im — maximum inclination of the Moon's orbit relative to Earth's equator ≈ 28.5°

Step 1 — Calculate the trigonometric factor

Governing equation
sin(2 im)
im — maximum inclination of the Moon's orbit to Earth's equator (= 28.5°)
sin(2im) — trigonometric factor (dimensionless)
Substitution
sin(2 × 28.5°) = sin(57°) ≈ 0.8387
Result of Step 1
sin(2im) ≈ 0.8387

Step 2 — Compute the secular inclination drift rate

The maximum long-term secular drift in inclination (di/dt) for a circular equatorial orbit due to a third body is governed by:

Governing equation
i̇max = 34 nm2n μm* sin(2 im)
i̇max — maximum secular rate of change of inclination (rad/s)
nm — Moon's mean motion (= 2.66 × 10−6 rad/s)
n — satellite mean motion (= 7.292 × 10−5 rad/s)
μm* — Moon's mass ratio (= 0.0123)
im — Moon's orbital inclination to the equator (= 28.5°)
3/4 — geometric coefficient for the third-body secular term
Substitution
i̇max = 34 ( 2.66 × 10−6 rad/s7.292 × 10−5 rad/s ) × 0.0123 × 0.8387

= 0.75 × (9.703 × 10−8) × 0.0123 × 0.8387
Result of Step 2
i̇max ≈ 7.51 × 10−10 rad/s

Step 3 — Convert to degrees per year

Governing equation
i̇max [°/yr] = i̇max [rad/s] × 180°π × (86 400 s/day × 365.25 days/year)
i̇max — drift rate (7.51 × 10−10 rad/s → °/yr)
180°/π — radians-to-degrees conversion (≈ 57.2958 °/rad)
86 400 s/day — seconds in one day
365.25 days/year — days in one Julian year
Substitution
i̇max ≈ 7.51 × 10−10 × 57.2958 × 31 557 600
  ≈ 7.51 × 10−10 × 1.808 × 109
Final answer
i̇max ≈ 1.36 °/year
Note: Combined with the Sun's gravity, this total lunisolar gravitational perturbation causes GEO satellite inclinations to drift by roughly 0.86° to 0.95° per year, requiring continuous north–south station-keeping.

a — semi-major axis (km)

Inclination

Equation
cos i = hzh
i — inclination (0°–180°)
hz — z-component of h⃗ (km2/s)
h — magnitude of h⃗ (km2/s)

Right Ascension of the Ascending Node (RAAN)

Equation
n⃗ = k̂ × h⃗ = (−hy, hx, 0)
Ω = atan2(ny, nx)
Ω — RAAN from vernal equinox eastward (rad or °)
n⃗ — node vector toward ascending node
k̂ — Earth's Z-axis unit vector (0,0,1)
hx, hy — components of h⃗ (km2/s)

Argument of Perigee

Equation
cos ω = n⃗ · e⃗|n⃗| |e⃗|
ω — argument of perigee (rad or °)
n⃗ — node vector
e⃗ — eccentricity vector (points to perigee)

Eccentricity vector

Equation
e⃗ = 1μ [ ( v2 − μr ) r⃗ − ( r⃗ · v⃗ ) v⃗ ]
e⃗ — eccentricity vector (dimensionless)
v — speed (km/s)
r — radius (km)
μ — gravitational parameter (km3/s2)

True Anomaly

Equation
cos ν = e⃗ · r⃗|e⃗| r
ν — true anomaly (rad or °); 0° at perigee, 180° at apogee
e⃗ — eccentricity vector
r⃗ — position vector (km)
r — position magnitude (km)

Radial velocity

Equation
vr = r⃗ · v⃗r
vr — radial velocity (km/s); <0 → approaching perigee
r⃗ · v⃗ — dot product (km2/s)
r — position magnitude (km)

🌀 Third-Body (Lunisolar) Perturbations

Maximum secular inclination drift (third body)

Equation
i̇max = 34 nm2n μm* sin(2 im)
i̇max — max secular drift rate (rad/s); GEO ≈ 7.51×10−10 rad/s
nm — Moon's mean motion (2.66×10−6 rad/s)
n — satellite mean motion (7.292×10−5 rad/s for GEO)
μm* — Moon mass ratio (0.0123)
im — Moon's orbit inclination to equator (28.5°)

Unit conversions for drift rates

Equation
i̇ [°/yr] = i̇ [rad/s] × 180°π × 86 400 × 365.25
180°/π — rad → deg (57.2958)
86 400 — seconds per day (s/day)
365.25 — days per Julian year (days/yr)
